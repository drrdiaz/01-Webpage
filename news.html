AI & Oncology

AI-Assisted Contouring in Radiation Oncology

Auto-segmentation tools powered by deep learning are now entering routine clinical use in radiation oncology departments worldwide. These tools can delineate organs at risk and target volumes in a fraction of the time required for manual contouring, with performance approaching that of experienced clinicians for well-defined structures such as the parotid glands, bladder, and femoral heads.

However, clinical implementation requires careful quality assurance. AI contours should always be reviewed and corrected by a qualified radiation oncologist before use in treatment planning. Current evidence suggests that AI contouring reduces inter-observer variability and planner workload, but does not yet replace the clinical judgement required for tumour delineation in complex cases.

The practical implication for radiation oncology practice is a shift in the role of the clinician — from drawing structures to reviewing and refining them. This demands training, standardised review workflows, and an understanding of where AI tools are most and least reliable.

Clinical Practice

SABR for Oligometastatic Disease: 2025 Update

Stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR) for oligometastatic disease has moved from hypothesis to established practice over the past decade. Landmark randomised trials — including SABR-COMET and ORIOLE — demonstrated survival benefits and improved progression-free survival in selected patients with limited metastatic burden.

More recent data from 2024–2025 have refined patient selection criteria. The definition of oligometastatic disease has evolved to incorporate not just the number of lesions (≤5 in most trials), but also biological factors such as disease-free interval, primary tumour histology, and response to systemic therapy. Patients with synchronous versus metachronous metastases appear to have different outcomes.

The practical implication is that SABR for oligometastatic disease should be discussed within a multidisciplinary setting, with clear documentation of intent (curative versus cytoreductive) and expectation management around the difference between progression-free and overall survival.

Radiotherapy in Regional Queensland

Access to radiation oncology services remains unequal across Australia. Patients in regional and remote areas face significant barriers including distance to treatment centres, accommodation costs, and the burden of daily travel during a course of treatment that may last several weeks.

Hypofractionation — delivering fewer, larger doses of radiation — has substantially reduced treatment duration for many common cancers, including prostate and breast cancer, making radiotherapy more feasible for patients who cannot be away from home for extended periods. SABR for early lung cancer, for instance, is delivered in 3–8 fractions rather than 30–35.

Telehealth consultations have also improved access to radiation oncology specialist advice without requiring patients to travel for every appointment. While the initial planning CT scan and treatment itself require attendance at a radiotherapy centre, many follow-up consultations and on-treatment reviews can now be conducted remotely.

As a radiation oncologist based in Rockhampton, equitable access to evidence-based radiotherapy for regional Queenslanders is a central focus of my practice.
